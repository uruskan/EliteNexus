# Elite Nexus — user guide

Unzip the release and run `EliteNexus.exe`. It finds your journal folder automatically
(Saved Games\Frontier Developments\Elite Dangerous), reads your whole history in well under a second, then follows
the game live. Run it before or after the game — order doesn't matter. On Linux, run `./EliteNexus`: it finds the
journals in the game's Proton prefix (see [Linux](#linux)). On a Mac, open `Elite Nexus.app`: it finds them in the
game's NotProton, CrossOver or Whisky prefix (see [macOS](#macos)).

Each start shows a small **support card** in the middle of the window: the GitHub
Sponsors and Buy Me a Coffee buttons (each opens its page in your browser), why support matters and what it pays
for, such as early-access ships to test in the game, and a thank-you to the supporters and contributors named in the
README (the release notes thank the same people). Elite Nexus is free, with no ads, and nothing in it depends on
support. After a 10-second countdown, **Close** appears; until then the card stays (Esc and clicks outside it don't
close it). The countdown runs only while you can see it: with Elite Nexus minimised or behind the game, it waits. It's only in the main window: never on the overlay or in VR, and it doesn't take focus from the game. On
a first start it comes after the welcome. A later version will skip it for supporters and everyone who bought a
coffee. Settings › About links GitHub Sponsors too.

## The window

The **sidebar** on the left lists every page; on narrow windows it shrinks to icons (hover for names). At its foot
is your commander: name, current system and a fuel gauge (hover for ship and situation, click for the Dashboard).

Each page has a large title, and the **toolbar** at the top right shows, left to right: your credits, the live
market feed (**EDDN n/min**: click it for the market database), uploads sent this session (once you enable a service;
amber = a service is paused or rejecting, click for details), **Pause uploads / Resume uploads** when you share with
EDDN, EDSM, Inara or EDAstro (**Pause EDDN** and so on when you share with only one): it pauses every service you
share with, or resumes them all, and what happens while they're paused is dropped, not sent later (market prices go
to EDDN only when you undock, so pausing while docked keeps a market you just found to yourself), and
**Live / Offline** (Live = the game is running and writing events). The round button next to it switches the
**overlay** on and off: a small black island at the top of the game's screen (see [Overlay](#overlay)). While it's on,
the lock beside it locks it (clicks go to the game) or unlocks it to move it.

A crash leaves no Shutdown in the game's journal, so Elite Nexus also looks for the game's program on this computer
(Windows and Linux). Once it has seen the game running and the program is gone, the toolbar says Offline, and the
overlay, Follow my flight, HUD & cockpit and Ask know the game has stopped. On Windows, with the journal folder Elite
Nexus finds by itself, it also knows when you start it after a crash: a game whose program isn't running is Offline at
once. Otherwise (a Mac, Linux, a journal folder shared from another computer), a game that has written nothing since
Elite Nexus started counts as stopped once neither its journal nor Status.json has been written for 15 minutes, until
it writes again (HUD & cockpit, which finishes a calibration of the cockpit lights when the game closes, waits for a
sure sign instead); a game that writes while Elite Nexus runs counts as running until it closes normally (unless its
program was seen here and has gone), because nothing else tells a crash from a long pause.

Alerts appear as banners at the top right and are spoken if you enable voice. The **bell** at the far right of the
toolbar keeps every alert since Elite Nexus started, newest first, with its time and what it said, so one you missed
while its banner was up can still be read; a number on the bell counts those that came in since you last opened it.
While the list is open no banners cover it: new alerts appear at its top. **Clear** empties it. It holds the last 200,
and only while Elite Nexus runs.

## Simple first, everything still there

Each page shows the one thing you usually need, with smart defaults (your current system, your ship's jump range and
landing pad). Searches show all their settings in a bar under the search, each with a short caption (hover it for
what it does and its limits): type a value, or step it with − and +. A k, M or B typed after a figure counts
thousands, millions or billions (50k is 50,000 Cr in a price alert; 500k is 0.5 in a field of M Cr), and a decimal
comma works too (19,6). Every Market tab, Exploration › Find valuable
bodies and the Navigation planners work this way, including everything the original tools offer, such as Spansh's
routing algorithm and neutron or injection choices. Elsewhere, advanced settings are one click away under **› Options**.

The trip goes to the nearest seller of one good, then the nearest seller of the next from there, or a shorter order when there is one: it's never longer than such a chain, and among the sellers it weighs no trip is shorter, unless so many are near enough to matter that it weighed the nearest only (the note under the stops says which). A station that sells several of the goods is one stop for all, and of two trips equally long the one with fewer stops wins. It uses Find commodity's landing pad, price age, fleet carrier and planetary filters, counts what's aboard towards each good (not mission cargo or stolen goods; a good that's all aboard drops out), and plans again when the list, the filters, your hold or your position change; until the new trip is in, the one before shows, and **Follow this trip** waits for it. Each stop says how far it is from you or from the stop before it, its pad, how far it lies from the star and how old its prices are, then what to buy there, at what price and with how much in stock. A good no seller has under the filters is listed with why: its nearest seller and every filter that leaves it out, as Find commodity says. It warns when what's still to buy of the amounts set is more than your free hold, or costs more than your credits. **Follow this trip** sends the stops to Navigation. In the pinned entry's system, a pinned fleet carrier shows **here** or **not here**: Elite Nexus checks the carriers the game listed since you arrived (the game lists them as they appear in your navigation panel: on arrival or when you drop out of supercruise; until it has, the pin says "not listed yet"). The list on the left finds any station or carrier the database knows by the start of its name (a carrier by its ID, such as J7F-10F), and lists the station you're docked at (or the last market you opened), your system's stations and the carriers the game listed since you arrived, then those the database still places there but the game didn't list ("not seen since you arrived"), and the markets you opened lately. Only stations and carriers with a market in the database are listed until you ask for the rest (a carrier the database has never had a market for has nothing to open); **Open all** puts your system's markets side by side, keeping the one you're docked at. A market you open in the game opens there by itself, first. Above the markets, **Filter goods** shows only the goods whose name or category holds what you type, in every market at once, and **Buy here** / **Sell here** only what the stations sell or buy. Where two or more open markets trade a good, the cheapest to buy and the best price to sell stand out on a tinted capsule. A column heading sorts every market the same way; a narrow market shows the prices, and stock, demand and the galactic average come back as it widens. The open markets are read again every ten seconds, so a new report from the live feed shows up; the list icon hides the list for more room. **Run planner** fills the pinned orders: for each pinned buyer it finds where to buy the goods (cargo already aboard first, then a pinned seller, then the nearest or cheapest seller within the distance you set, combining up to three small sellers), for each pinned seller it finds the best buyer, fits your free hold space and credits (most profit per tonne first) and orders the stops so everything is bought before it's sold, on the shortest straight-line trip. It shows profit, spend, stops and what was left out and why; **Follow this run** puts the stops on the Navigation page. **Abnormal prices** scans the whole database for stations and fleet carriers paying far more than a commodity's galactic average (sell there) or selling far below it (buy there), e.g. a carrier buy order paying 100× the normal price. Pick the multiple (1.5× to 10×), the whole galaxy or a radius, and how old prices may be; it updates as you change them. Carriers report no average, so they are compared with what stations report for that commodity. **Sell my cargo** (best single stop for your whole hold, re-planned after every jump, and the best buyer of each commodity with its station, system, distance and demand; the bar above sets how far to look and how old prices may be, and shows the pad your ship needs), trade routes for a cargo space and your credits (**Cargo space** follows your ship's hold until you set another, to check routes for a trade ship you're not flying (set its landing pad too); the one you set is remembered, and the ↺ that then shows beside it goes back to your ship's; within up to 1,000 ly, hops up to 500 ly; wide areas take a few seconds; each route shows the stock where you buy and the demand where you sell, and its pin puts both ends in the Run planner, buying at the start and selling at the end (a second click unpins both; with no room for both under the 24-pin limit, neither pins); docked, **From this station** lists only the routes that buy where you are, sold within the distance you set: open the commodity market in the game first, so Elite Nexus has that station's latest prices), nearest material traders / brokers / Interstellar Factors / Vista Genomics (**Services** searches as soon as it opens and again after every jump: the 25 nearest stations with the service out of every station Elite Nexus knows, with no distance limit, so one 3,000 ly away is listed once the nearer ones are, and **Show 25 more** reaches further; how far that goes depends on what the database knows: with the live feed alone, the stations commanders reported lately and the ones you visited, after the Spansh import every station in the galaxy. The bar above sets the landing pad, and whether fleet carriers, left out by default because they move, and planetary ports count), mining hotspots + best sell price, **price alerts** ("tell me when a station pays 60,000+ for Tritium within 50 ly", spoken seconds after any commander docks there; type the price or step it with − and +, by 1,000 Cr around 48,000 Cr and by 100,000 Cr around 1.5 M Cr; **Within** can be **Anywhere**; under the new alert, its galactic average and the best price in its range right now set the price in one click). | Mining | Live session stats, earlier sessions, and for Rhino surface mining the sites you worked, a surface radar and where to mine next | Tonnes/hour, prospect quality, motherlodes. **Surface mining sites** lists every planetary mining location the Rhino refined at, newest first: the body and the number the game's navigation panel shows for it (Planetary Mining Location 4), the system (copy it, or plot a route there), what the refinery made there by commodity (by-products included), visits and the last visit, how many mining locations your scans found on that body, and a note of your own. Pick a commodity to see only the locations that gave it. To go back, jump to the system, target the body and pick that location in the navigation panel. The number is the location the game names nearest to where you land your ship, read from the journal, so runs from before you installed this version are listed too. It holds while you send the ship away from the Rhino and call it back (the game writes those lift-offs and landings without a position), and ends when you lift off yourself. The game names a location only when your ship touches down or lifts off within 50 km of one. When you drop the Rhino from a hovering ship, Elite Nexus takes the number of the mining location you had targeted instead (from Status.json, so only while it's running) and keeps it for later starts. A run with neither, or after the ship set down further away, says "location not named", and the body's count of locations you've worked says "at least": target the location, or touch down by it, before you deploy the Rhino; if you know the number, keep it in the note. Live and earlier sessions say where they were. The **Surface radar** appears near a body you've mined (from orbit, in glide and on the ground): "up" is the way you're facing, and it shows the deposits the Rhino refined at, your ship, and where it landed last time, each with its distance and compass bearing (turn until your heading shows it). It's scaled to what's near you (deposits, your ship, where it landed), so one deposit far away doesn't squeeze the rest into the middle: anything further out waits on the rim, in its direction, with its distance. The overlay's radar uses the same scale and keeps anything further out at its edge. Deposit positions come from Status.json while Elite Nexus is running, because the journal records none, and are kept between runs. Rigs aren't in the journal either, so once the Rhino has refined at two places on a deposit, a ring round them shows the ground it worked there: your rigs most likely stood within it, and the deposit may reach further. Beside the radar, a north-up **minimap** frames the deposits near you with their rings and the places the Rhino refined, your ship, where it landed and you, with a scale bar (75 m, the rig spacing, when it's zoomed in that far). **Placing rigs**, beside them (or on its own while you drive the Rhino before your first deposit), says how far apart rigs go and how many fit: about 75 m apart and, commanders report, up to six at once, so a round deposit takes 2 rigs from 75 m across, 3 from 87 m, 4 from 106 m, 5 from 128 m and 6 from 150 m (one in the middle, five on a ring 75 m out). Frontier hasn't published the spacing: 75 m is what commanders reported in September 2026 (their estimates ran from 75 to 100 m, and they report that the game refuses a rig closer), and the page says so. Deposits are numbered on each body in the order the Rhino records them (those kept by an earlier version oldest first), and keep their numbers; each one in the list under the radar takes a note of your own, such as how many rigs fit, so two deposits of the same commodity can be told apart. Landing by a location you mined before shows a quiet reminder of what you got there. **Where to mine** (the page's second tab) finds bodies worth landing on with the Rhino. **Near you** asks Spansh for landable bodies with planetary mining locations within 25 to 1,000 ly: the 500 nearest, sorted nearest first or most locations first, each with its ground, its number of mining locations, its distance from the star, its gravity and when a scan last reported it; "Mined" marks a body you've worked. A body's **ground** is its class (metal-rich, high metal content, rocky, rocky ice or icy) and its volcanism (none, metallic or rocky magma, silicate vapour geysers, water magma or geysers, carbon dioxide geysers, ammonia, methane or nitrogen magma), as the game reports them; pick a class, a volcanism or both, and a least number of locations, to narrow the search (under Options, a largest distance from the star). Pick a **Material** (Monazite, Platinum, Iridium…) for the odds of finding it: the grounds where it turned up, each with the share of the mining locations read there that listed it (Monazite: 58 % of rocky magma locations, 41 % of metallic magma ones, 3 % of rocky ones without volcanism, 1 % with silicate vapour geysers, and none of the 615 read on metal-rich, high metal content, rocky ice or icy bodies); click one to search that ground. With no class picked, the search asks Spansh only for the classes where it turned up. Each body then shows its odds and about how many of its own locations would list it ("≈ 8 of 14", an average), and **Best odds** sorts by that; along a route, each stretch's stop is the system with the most locations likely to list it. ## Overlay

The overlay is a small black island at the top centre of the game's screen, in a window of its own beside the main
window. It says what's going on in a word or two, opens up for the moments that need you, and fades away when nothing
does. Switch it on with the round button in the toolbar or under Settings › Overlay.

What it shows depends on what you're doing:

* **Next jump**: in supercruise with a route, the next star and whether you can scoop it, and the jumps left with
  the straight-line distance to where the route ends ("Scoopable · 12 jumps · 231 ly"). It opens while the FSD
  charges a hyperspace jump, in hyperspace (the star you're arriving at) and in the galaxy map: the next system's
  name (on your clipboard when "copy next system" is on), the next neutron star on the route, your fuel, and the
  route's last system with how far it is ("Orrere · 23.4 ly away", or "Last jump of the route"). The route is the
  one plotted in the galaxy map, or one planned on Navigation; a planned stop Spansh gave no position for shows
  without a distance.
* **Arrival**: it opens for five seconds when you arrive somewhere worth it (a body worth at least the high-value
  setting, biology, an Earth-like, water or ammonia world, or a first discovery) and stays open while the FSS, system
  map or orrery is; then it settles to the bodies with biology and what the system is worth once mapped.
* **Approach**: open in orbital cruise and while gliding: the body's gravity, atmosphere and temperature, the genera
  there (struck through once analysed), what its biology is likely worth (×5 when the first-logged bonus is likely)
  and, when Elite Nexus knows one, where to land: the settlement you're approaching, the nearest deposit the Rhino
  refined at, or where your ship set down by a mining location, with distance and compass bearing. On the surface it
  stays small (bio signals and gravity); while you map the body, its mapped value, then its genera.
* **Sampling**: "2 of 3 samples" and whether you're far enough from your earlier samples for the next one to count:
  "Far enough" in green, or "48 m to go" in yellow. It opens for three seconds when you take a sample and when you get
  far enough, with a radar of your earlier samples and their colony ranges, turned so that up is the way you're
  facing. Positions come from Status.json while Elite Nexus runs; samples taken without it have none, and it says so.
* **Rhino mining**: in the Rhino near deposits it refined at, the nearest deposit (its number on the body and its
  mining location), how far, and which way to turn. It opens when you reach a deposit and when you turn back to your
  ship, with the radar of numbered deposits, your ship and where it landed.
* **Trade stop**: a market you pinned in this system. A pinned fleet carrier shows its callsign (the navigation panel
  lists carriers by it) and whether the game has listed it here yet. It opens when you drop out of supercruise there:
  what to buy or sell, how much, the price when you pinned it, and the route's next stop.
* **Docking**: when a station grants docking, it opens for four seconds with your landing pad and the station (once
  you close the panel you asked from), then keeps the pad ("Pad 36") until you dock, the permission runs out or you
  leave. A denied request opens it too, with the reason in plain words ("too far away: ask again within 7.5 km").
* **Docked**: it opens for six seconds as you dock: the exploration and exobiology data this station buys, and what
  it's worth, and orders you pinned here.
* **Carrier**: your carrier's jump countdown ("Carrier → Colonia 7:42"); when something else is going on, in a small
  capsule of its own beside the island.
* **Alerts**: an important alert opens the island for four seconds, then it goes back to what it was. One that comes
  while a cockpit panel is open waits until the panel closes.

While a cockpit panel or the station's menus are open it's a small empty capsule, and in danger or while being
interdicted it disappears (only a critical alert shows). It shows only while Elite Dangerous or Elite Nexus is the
window in front; **Visible over every window** keeps it. When there's nothing to say it fades away; **Keep it when
idle** keeps the small capsule.

**Locked** (the lock in the toolbar, or Settings › Overlay), clicks go through it to the game and it never takes the
focus, so the game keeps your keyboard and joystick. Unlock it to drag it elsewhere; a click on it then opens its page
in the main window. **Where** puts it at the top left, centre or right of the game's screen; **Size** scales it
(100 to 250 %), whatever size the main window uses.

**See-through** draws only the island, and the game shows all around it. The island has a soft shadow and changes
shape as it opens and closes. It applies after a restart. Off, the island is an opaque window of its own that fits
what it shows: a small capsule for a word or two, larger only while it's open, in the same place as the see-through
island. It switches size instead of changing shape, and its content crossfades. On Windows that window has the
island's rounded shape; on Linux and macOS its corners are square. Changing size costs nothing you'd notice: the
window is resized only when the island changes shape, and nothing is drawn while it's still. On Windows, see-through
uses DirectX 12 through DirectComposition; on a PC without DirectX 12 the island stays its own opaque window, and
Settings › Overlay says so.

What each system can do:

* **Windows**: in exclusive **Fullscreen** the game hides every other window, the overlay too: set Options ›
  Graphics › Display to **Borderless** or **Windowed**.
* **Linux**: on X11 (and XWayland) it stays on top and knows which window is in front; see-through needs a compositing
  window manager running when Elite Nexus starts, and without one the island stays its own opaque window and Settings
  › Overlay says so. Wayland lets no app keep a window on top or say which one is in front: start Elite Nexus as
  `WAYLAND_DISPLAY= ./EliteNexus` to run it through XWayland.
* **macOS**: a game in full screen has a Space of its own, where other apps' windows don't show: run the game in a
  window. macOS doesn't say which window is in front, so the overlay always shows; with see-through off, macOS may
  bring the overlay to the front each time it appears.

### VR

**VR mode** (Settings › Overlay) makes the overlay an opaque black panel of fixed size that never hides, with nothing
smaller than 14 pt, so a tool that mirrors desktop windows into your headset always has it to show. Elite Nexus draws
nothing inside VR itself. Tools that mirror a window ("Elite Nexus overlay"):

* **Desktop+** (free), **OVR Toolkit** and **XSOverlay** (both paid), with the game in SteamVR: add the window and pin
  it to your view or to a place in the cockpit.
* **SteamVR's own desktop view**, from the SteamVR dashboard.
* **OpenKneeboard**, in its SteamVR mode.
* With the game in **Oculus** mode (no SteamVR), Meta Quest Link's **Pin to Experience** pins a desktop window inside
  the game.
* On Linux, **WayVR**.

**Alerts in XSOverlay** (Settings › Overlay, off by default): the alerts that open the island also appear as XSOverlay
notifications in your headset, silently (Elite Nexus speaks them itself if voice is on). They go only to XSOverlay
on this computer, through its notification API (UDP port 42069); with XSOverlay closed they're simply dropped.

## Voice

Alerts are spoken through Windows speech (on Linux through speech-dispatcher's `spd-say`, which most desktop
distributions include, or `espeak-ng` / `espeak` without it; with none of them alerts are shown, not spoken; on macOS
through the system's `say`). Settings › **Test voice** says which one spoke, or why none could.

On Windows, Settings › **Speech engine** chooses between **Windows speech** (the default: the voices of Windows
Settings › Time & language › Speech) and **SAPI** (Windows' classic speech engine: its desktop voices and the voices
other programs install for it, such as IVONA or CereProc, which Windows speech doesn't list). **Voice** picks one of
the engine's voices; **Default** follows the voice chosen in Windows. Switching the engine goes back to its default
voice, since the two engines have different voices. If the chosen voice is uninstalled later, the default voice
speaks instead and Settings says so. A voice you install while Elite Nexus runs shows up in the list when you open
Settings or the list. With SAPI, a new speed or volume applies at once, even to a line being spoken, and a new voice
from the next line; lines waiting to be spoken keep their turn.

Choose the minimum priority in Settings:
* **Critical** — hull critical, ship destroyed.
* **High** — Earth-like/water/ammonia/terraformable/high-value bodies, motherlodes, analysis complete, massacre
  mission done, fuel low with a non-scoopable star next, interdiction.
* **Normal** — biological signals, sample 1 logged (+ distance to move), codex entries, carrier jump scheduled.
* **Low** — arrival announcements, discovery-scan body counts, system fully scanned.

## Data & privacy

Everything lives in `nexus-data\` next to the executable (settings, galaxy database, saved builds, logs). Nothing is
uploaded unless you enable it in Accounts & sync; when you do, only live game data is sent (never your history,
credits or cargo), following each service's published rules.

On Linux, when the folder next to the program can't be written to, data goes to `~/.local/share/EliteNexus` (or
`$XDG_DATA_HOME/EliteNexus`). On macOS, `Elite Nexus.app` keeps its data in `~/Library/Application Support/EliteNexus`
(never inside the app, so replacing the app keeps it).

**AI agents** (Settings › AI agents, off by default): while it's on, an AI program on this computer that you set up
with Elite Nexus can read what Ask reads: your commander (location, ship, credits, ranks and their progress,
reputation, engineers, Powerplay, squadron), your fleet and its engineering, cargo, materials and on-foot gear, your
credit history and the factions you met, your journal (any period), the market database, the engineering data, and
what Spansh and EDGalaxyData answer the tools on its behalf. It can't change anything in the game or in Elite Nexus.
What the agent does with what it reads is up to that program: one that uses a cloud model sends it to that model's
company, as Ask does to yours. Other computers can't reach it, and on this computer only programs running under your
account can (or an administrator, who can reach anything). On Windows the pipe is named after your account and only
your account may open it; the program the agent starts uses it only when your account owns it, and lets Elite Nexus
learn who it is but not act as you. On Linux and macOS the socket is in a folder only your account can enter
(`/run/user/<your id>`, or `~/.elite-nexus`), and each end checks that the other runs as you.

Your settings are in `settings.json` in the data folder. If that file can't be read (typically after editing it by
hand: JSON writes every backslash twice, so a network folder is `"\\\\MAINPC\\Elite Dangerous"`), Elite Nexus never
starts over silently. It keeps the file as it was (`settings-unreadable-<date>.json`, next to it), keeps every
setting that still reads (from a file that isn't JSON at all, the EDSM and Inara keys, and the Ask key with the
service it belongs to; uploads stay off until you switch them on again) and says so in a banner above every page. A
file it can't open at all, say one another program is holding, isn't saved over until the next start. Settings ›
Journal › **Browse…** sets folders without editing the file.

If Elite Nexus closes unexpectedly, it writes where to `crash.log` in the data folder (Settings › About › Data folder
› **Open**), and the next start says it didn't close normally. `nexus.log` there shows what it was doing. Neither file
is sent anywhere: attaching them to a GitHub issue or a Frontier Forums post helps fix the problem. A journal entry
Elite Nexus can't handle is skipped (and listed under Settings › Journal) instead of stopping it from following the
game. Start it from a folder you unzipped it into: run from inside the zip, Windows unpacks it to a new temporary
folder each time, settings and database included, and Elite Nexus says so.

## Linux

Elite Nexus runs natively on 64-bit Linux: unpack `EliteNexus-…-linux-x64.tar.gz` anywhere and run `./EliteNexus`.
The game itself runs under Proton (Steam) or Wine (Lutris, Heroic), and writes its journals and settings inside that
Wine prefix, in the same folders as on Windows; Elite Nexus reads them there. The Linux build is new: it is built and
tested on Ubuntu, and a commander has confirmed it with the game under Proton (Nobara Linux 44 on Wayland: the journals
were found by themselves, Exploration and Exobiology work). HUD colours and Cockpit lights haven't been confirmed there
yet. Reports are collected in GitHub discussion #17 (github.com/uruskan/EliteNexus/discussions/17).

* **Journal folder**: found automatically in the game's Proton prefix in every Steam library (native, Flatpak and
  Snap Steam): `steamapps/compatdata/359320/pfx/drive_c/users/steamuser/Saved Games/Frontier Developments/Elite
  Dangerous`. Then in Lutris's (`~/Games/<game>`) and Heroic's (`~/Games/Heroic/Prefixes/default/<game>`) usual
  prefixes and `~/.wine`. For any other prefix, enter the folder under **Settings › Journal › Custom journal folder**.
* **Bindings, HUD colours, Glare and exposure** use the game's Options folder in the same prefix
  (`…/drive_c/users/<name>/AppData/Local/Frontier Developments/Elite Dangerous/Options`), so they work as on Windows.
* **The game folder** (built-in control presets, Cockpit lights) is found in your Steam libraries, and in Program
  Files inside the game's prefix for a Frontier or Epic launcher run under Wine. Anywhere else, set it under
  **Settings › Game**.
* **Cockpit lights** and **Keep hostile red, friendly green and shield blue** use 3Dmigoto, which is untested under
  Proton. If calibrating finds no shaders, add `WINEDLLOVERRIDES="d3d11,d3dcompiler_47=n,b" %command%` to the
  game's launch options in Steam.
* **App menu**: Settings › About › **App menu** › **Add** puts Elite Nexus and its icon in your desktop's app menu
  (`~/.local/share/applications/elite-nexus.desktop` and icons under `~/.local/share/icons`, no root needed); Wayland
  docks then show its icon too. From a terminal: `./EliteNexus --install-desktop` (`--uninstall-desktop` takes it
  out). Move the program and the row says the menu still starts the old copy, with **Point it here**.
* **Voice** uses `spd-say` (speech-dispatcher), else `espeak-ng` or `espeak`. Under **Test voice**, Settings says
  which one spoke, that none is installed, or what went wrong (speech-dispatcher also needs an output module, such as
  `speech-dispatcher-espeak-ng` on Fedora and Nobara). **API keys** aren't encrypted (Linux has no DPAPI); the settings
  file that holds them is readable only by your user account.
* The window draws with Vulkan or OpenGL, on X11 or Wayland. On Wayland the overlay can't keep itself above other
  windows (Wayland doesn't let apps do that): start Elite Nexus as `WAYLAND_DISPLAY= ./EliteNexus` to run it through
  XWayland, where it can.

## macOS

Elite Nexus runs natively on macOS (Apple Silicon and Intel) as `Elite Nexus.app`, next to Elite Dangerous running
under NotProton (Steam Play for the macOS Steam client), CrossOver or Whisky. On a Mac with Apple Silicon, download
`EliteNexus-…-macos-arm64.zip` from the latest release, unzip it and drag `Elite Nexus.app` to Applications. The app
isn't notarized by Apple (that needs a paid developer account), so the first time you open it macOS refuses: open
System Settings › Privacy & Security and click **Open Anyway** next to Elite Nexus, once. On an Intel Mac, build it
from the source with `tools/macos/build-app.sh --install` (it needs Xcode's Command Line Tools and Rust), which puts
the app in Applications. The macOS build is new and hasn't been tried by us with every way of running the game.

* **Journal folder**: found automatically in the game's prefix in every Steam library, which is where NotProton keeps
  it, the same layout as Proton's: `~/Library/Application Support/Steam/steamapps/compatdata/359320/pfx/drive_c/users/steamuser/Saved Games/Frontier Developments/Elite Dangerous`.
  Then in CrossOver's bottles (`~/Library/Application Support/CrossOver/Bottles/<bottle>`) and `~/.wine`, and only
  when none of those has one, in Whisky's bottles (they sit in Whisky's own container, so macOS asks once whether
  Elite Nexus may look there). For any other prefix (Sikarugir, a Wineskin wrapper), enter the folder under
  **Settings › Journal › Custom journal folder**.
* **Bindings, HUD colours, Glare and exposure** use the game's Options folder in the same prefix, as on Linux.
* **The game folder** (built-in control presets, Cockpit lights) is found in your Steam libraries
  (`~/Library/Application Support/Steam/steamapps/common/Elite Dangerous`) and in Program Files inside the prefix.
* **Cockpit lights** and **Keep hostile red, friendly green and shield blue** use 3Dmigoto, which is untested on
  macOS: CrossOver runs Direct3D 11 through D3DMetal or DXVK. If calibrating finds no shaders, add
  `WINEDLLOVERRIDES="d3d11,d3dcompiler_47=n,b" %command%` to the game's launch options in Steam; if it still finds
  none, the hook can't run there, and **Remove** takes it out again.
* **Voice** uses the system's `say`, in the system voice chosen in System Settings › Accessibility › Spoken
  Content. **API keys** aren't encrypted (there's no DPAPI); the settings file that holds them is readable only by
  your user account.
* The window draws with Metal. The overlay stays above other windows, but macOS gives a full-screen app a Space of
  its own, where other apps' windows don't show: run the game in a window.
